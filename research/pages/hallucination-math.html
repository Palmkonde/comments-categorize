Hallucination, heuristics and JEV, explained with math

2026-11-06 · Companion to hallucination-mitigation-vs-jev.md · Facts about JEV come from TypeSafe's docs and one preprint. Anything they do not document is marked not documented.

  • A generative LLM samples from the whole space of strings \(V^*\). Nothing in the math keeps the output inside the set of true answers.
  • Hallucination is classification error made larger by generation: \(\text{err}_{\text{gen}} \gtrsim 2\,\text{err}_{\text{IIV}}\). Benchmarks that score "I don't know" as 0 make guessing the best strategy.
  • A heuristic is a cheap \(h \approx f\) that is right most of the time and comes with no guarantee.
  • JEV outputs a distribution \(\pi\) over a finite label set \(Y\) that you define, so \(P(\text{output} \notin Y) = 0\). Wrong labels \(1-\pi(y^*)\) and miscalibration remain.
  • On TypeSafe's own eval, JEV is on the accuracy-per-cost Pareto front: about the same accuracy as terra at 76× lower cost. It is not the most accurate (67.8% vs 74.1%). No evidence shows it is SoTA.

1. What a generative LLM computes

\[ p_\theta(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \prod_{t=1}^{n} p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}), \qquad p_\theta(x_t = i \mid x_{\lt t}) = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j \in V} e^{z_j}} \] \[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_t \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) \quad\text{(next-token cross-entropy)} \]

Here \(V\) is the vocabulary (about 100k tokens) and \(z\) is the logit vector. Let \(A(x)\) be the set of valid, true answers to prompt \(x\). Then:

\[ \text{hallucination rate}(x) = P_{y \sim p_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\big[\, y \notin A(x) \,\big] \]

The loss rewards text that looks like the training data. It does not reward text that is true. The output space is all strings \(V^*\), so nothing in the math forces \(y \in A(x)\).

2. Why hallucination happens

Source: Kalai et al. 2025, "Why language models hallucinate" (OpenAI); Kalai & Vempala 2023.

2.1 Lower bound from classification error

Kalai et al. turn generation into a yes/no question, "Is-It-Valid" (IIV): is this string valid? They show:

\[ \text{err}_{\text{generative}} \;\gtrsim\; 2 \cdot \text{err}_{\text{IIV}} \]

A model that cannot tell valid output from invalid output must also generate invalid output. The exact statement (Theorem 1, §3.2) has two extra terms:

\[ \text{err} \;\ge\; 2\cdot\text{err}_{\text{iiv}} \;-\; \frac{\max_c |V_c|}{\min_c |E_c|} \;-\; \delta \]

\(V_c\) and \(E_c\) are the valid and error responses for prompt \(c\). \(\delta\) is the model's calibration gap. Kalai et al. say the constant 2 "is relatively tight".

2.2 Rare facts (singleton rate)

Some facts, such as a person's birthday, follow no pattern. For these facts:

\[ \text{hallucination rate} \;\gtrsim\; \text{singleton rate} = \frac{\#\{\text{facts seen exactly once in training}\}}{\#\{\text{facts}\}} \]

Kalai et al. give this example: "if 20% of birthday facts appear exactly once in the pretraining data, then one expects base models to hallucinate on at least 20% of birthday facts" (§1.1; formal version in Theorem 2, §3.3.1).

2.3 Benchmarks reward guessing

Most benchmarks score right = 1, wrong = 0 and abstain = 0. Let \(p\) be the chance a guess is right:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{score} \mid \text{guess}] = p,\qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{score} \mid \text{abstain}] = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \text{guess whenever } p \gt 0 \]

Kalai et al. propose a fix: pick a confidence target \(t\), then score right = \(+1\), wrong = \(-\tfrac{t}{1-t}\), abstain = \(0\):

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{guess}] = p - (1-p)\frac{t}{1-t} \gt 0 \iff p \gt t \]

With this scoring the best strategy is to answer only when \(p \gt t\), so abstaining becomes rational. Kalai et al. (§4.2): "the expected score of offering an answer beats IDK (score 0) iff its confidence (i.e., probability of being correct) is >t."

3. How LLMs and SLMs reduce hallucination

MethodMathWhat it doesLimit
Temperature \(p_i(T) = \dfrac{e^{z_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}}\) \(T \to 0\) gives greedy decoding. Lower \(T\) means fewer rare tokens. Greedy decoding can still be confidently wrong.
Top-p / nucleus
Holtzman 2019		 \(S = \min\{S : \sum_{i \in S} p_i \ge p\}\) Samples only from the high-probability set \(S\) and cuts the tail. A heuristic: a likely token can still be false.
RAG
Lewis 2020		 \(p(y \mid x) = \sum_z p_\eta(z \mid x)\, p_\theta(y \mid x, z)\) Grounds the answer in a retrieved document \(z\), not only in the weights. Bad retrieval leads to a bad answer. Matters most for SLMs.
Contrastive decoding / DoLa
Li 2022, Chuang 2023		 \(s(i) = \log p_{\text{strong}}(i) - \log p_{\text{weak}}(i)\) DoLa contrasts later and earlier layers to bring out factual knowledge. Contrastive decoding contrasts a large and a small model, and was built for generation quality, not mainly for factuality. Needs access to the model's internals.
Semantic entropy
Farquhar 2024		 \(SE(x) = -\sum_c p(c \mid x) \log p(c \mid x)\)
\(p(c \mid x) = \sum_{s \in c} p(s \mid x)\)		 Samples \(N\) answers and groups them by meaning into clusters \(c\). High \(SE\) means the model is confabulating, so it should abstain. Costs \(N\) samples. Detects errors but does not fix them.
Self-consistency / SelfCheckGPT
Wang 2022, Manakul 2023		 \(\text{agree} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k \mathbb{1}[y_k = \hat y]\) Low agreement between samples signals a likely hallucination. Consistent answers can still be consistently wrong.
Constrained decoding
Willard & Louf 2023, Geng 2023		 \(z_i \leftarrow -\infty\) if token \(i\) breaks the grammar The output always matches the schema (JSON, labels). Matches the schema, but is not guaranteed true. Closest relative of JEV.
Calibration + abstention
Guo 2017, Geifman 2017		 Answer iff \(\text{conf} \ge \tau\) Higher \(\tau\) means fewer errors but fewer answers. Only works if the confidence is calibrated. RLHF can hurt calibration (GPT-4 report, Fig. 8).
Honesty / abstention training
R-Tuning, Zhang 2023		 Reward "I don't know" when \(p \lt t\) (section 2.3) Teaches the model to refuse when it is unsure. Can make the model refuse too often.

Calibration

A model is calibrated if, of all answers it gives confidence \(q\), a fraction \(q\) are right. Expected calibration error (ECE) measures the gap. It sorts predictions into confidence bins \(B_b\):

\[ \text{calibrated} \iff P(\text{correct} \mid \text{conf} = q) = q \;\;\forall q, \qquad \text{ECE} = \sum_{b} \frac{|B_b|}{n}\,\big|\text{acc}(B_b) - \text{conf}(B_b)\big| \]

4. What "heuristic" means for language models

Tversky and Kahneman: heuristics "are quite useful, but sometimes they lead to severe and systematic errors" (Science, 1974). In function terms (my formalisation, not a quote), a heuristic is a cheap function \(h\) that approximates the true function \(f\):

\[ P_{x \sim D_{\text{train}}}[h(x) = f(x)] \text{ high},\quad \text{cost}(h) \ll \text{cost}(f),\quad \text{no guarantee when } D_{\text{test}} \ne D_{\text{train}} \]
SenseMathExample
(a) Shortcut learning
McCoy 2019		 \(P_{\text{train}}(y \mid s) \approx 1\), but \(P_{\text{test}}(y \mid s) \approx\) base rate "High word overlap means entailment" works on training data and fails on new data.
(b) Decoding heuristics \(\arg\max_i p_i\), top-k, top-p, \(T\) Rules for picking the next token.
(c) Heuristic detectors Flag if \(\frac{1}{n}\log p_\theta(y) \lt \tau\) Low log-probability or low n-gram overlap with the source signals a hallucination.
(d) System 1 (Kahneman) Fast \(h\) used in place of slow, multi-step \(f\) TypeSafe's name "System One model" refers to this sense.

5. How JEV works, as math

The request defines a finite output space \(Y\). JEV returns a distribution over \(Y\) and never generates text (TypeSafe blog):

5.1 Why JEV "cannot hallucinate"

\[ \operatorname{supp}(\pi) \subseteq Y \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; P(\text{output} \notin Y) = 0 \]

This is TypeSafe's "0%" figure. It holds by construction and is not measured. TypeSafe itself says the number is "not empirical".

5.2 The error that remains

\[ \text{risk} = \mathbb{E}_x\big[\,1 - \pi_x(y^*)\,\big] \qquad (y^* = \text{correct label}) \]

Kalai's bound says generative error comes from classification error. JEV removes the generation step, so only the classification error is left. A fixed label set changes the form of a mistake, not how often mistakes happen.

Calibration alone is not enough. A model that always outputs the base rate is perfectly calibrated (\(\text{ECE} = 0\)) but useless. You need discrimination metrics (accuracy, AUROC) as well. The preprint gives an example of the gap: on prompt injection, JEV had AUROC 0.982 but recall of only 50% (Deußer et al. 2026).

Not documented: the architecture, parameter count, training data, the RLCD ("Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions") objective, and how the choice and score confidences are computed.

7. How to set a review band from costs

This section is my own derivation. It applies the expected-cost decision principle (Elkan 2001) to three actions. The cost values are illustrative.

Let \(p\) be a calibrated probability that an item is positive, for example a JEV noul. There are three actions:

  • act: treat the item as positive automatically
  • pass: treat it as negative automatically
  • defer: send it to a person or a slower system

Define three costs:

  • \(c_{FP}\): acting on an item that was actually negative
  • \(c_{FN}\): passing an item that was actually positive
  • \(c_H\): one deferral

The expected cost of each action:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{act}] = (1-p)\,c_{FP},\qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{pass}] = p\,c_{FN},\qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{defer}] = c_H \] \[ \text{act if } p \gt 1 - \frac{c_H}{c_{FP}},\qquad \text{pass if } p \lt \frac{c_H}{c_{FN}},\qquad \text{otherwise defer} \]
Cost assumptionPass belowAct aboveMeaning
\(c_{FP} = c_{FN} = 3.33\,c_H\)0.300.70TypeSafe's suggested start (docs) implies an error costs about 3.3 deferrals
\(c_{FP} = c_{FN} = 4\,c_H\)0.250.75Symmetric errors, each worth 4 deferrals
\(c_{FP} = 10\,c_H,\ c_{FN} = 4\,c_H\)0.250.90A false action is the expensive mistake (for example, blocking a legitimate user)
\(c_{FP} = 4\,c_H,\ c_{FN} = 20\,c_H\)0.050.75A miss is the expensive mistake (for example, a security alert)
\(c_H \ge c_{FP}c_{FN}/(c_{FP}+c_{FN})\)no bandDeferring never pays. Use one threshold, \(p^* = c_{FP}/(c_{FP}+c_{FN})\).

This reasoning is only valid if \(p\) is calibrated. To check that, hand-label a sample of your items and measure ECE.

8.3 Can small evals show a winner?

\[ SE(\widehat{\text{acc}}) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}},\qquad n = 200,\ p = 0.7 \;\Rightarrow\; SE \approx 0.032,\quad 95\%\ \text{CI} \approx \pm 6.4 \text{ points} \]

An eval of 200 items therefore cannot separate two models less than about 6 points apart. TypeSafe does not publish \(n\) for its eval in the material found, so its 6.3-point gap cannot be tested for significance.
